I. Executive Summary

This report presents the second in a series of empirical studies evaluating the llms.txt standard — a structured guide file that institutions publish so AI systems can read and use their information. Our research question: does giving an AI assistant this guide improve how accurately it answers questions about the institution — and does it do so uniformly, or only for questions the guide actually covers?

We ran 14,500 structured test questions (fact-probe queries) across five institutions, five AI models, and twelve experimental conditions, using GPT-4o-mini as an automated accuracy judge. The study yields five principal findings:

  1. llms.txt V2 appears to work for covered facts. Giving the AI a complete, well-structured V2 guide improved factual accuracy on covered facts by +60.6 percentage points averaged across all five institutions (p<.001, Cohen's h=+1.452 — a large effect by standard statistical measures), though sample size limits how broadly this can be generalized.
  2. Incomplete coverage may actively harm accuracy. On facts not covered by the guide, giving the AI the guide reduced accuracy by −6.4 percentage points (p=0.001) in our experiments. This suggests the AI may perform worse on questions outside the guide's scope than if no guide had been provided at all.
  3. Prompt-level fixes did not work in our tests. Instructing the AI to use the guide only when relevant — three variants tested — failed to recover accuracy on uncovered facts and in some cases made it worse.
  4. Query-time gating looks promising. Applying a relevance filter (Retrieval-Augmented Generation, or RAG — a standard technique that checks whether a question matches a document before showing it to the AI) to llms.txt reduced the out-of-scope accuracy drop while preserving most of the accuracy benefit on covered facts. More testing is needed across more institutions.
  5. The automated judge agrees moderately with humans and exhibits in-family bias. A pre-registered human-validation sub-study (n=1,000 ratings from 5 independent graders) places majority-vote agreement between humans and the GPT-4o-mini judge at κ=0.570 — in the "moderate" band of the Landis & Koch scale. Agreement varies systematically by target model family: κ=0.575 on GPT-family outputs vs. κ=0.324 on Llama outputs, a ~0.25 gap larger than the standard error of the overall estimate. Per-model accuracy rankings reported above should be interpreted with this caveat.
Preliminary implication: An institution that publishes an llms.txt covering only part of its operations may find that guide harms AI accuracy on the topics it does not cover. A relevance filter applied before the guide is shown to the AI appears to help. Adding covers: and excludes: fields to the llms.txt spec could make this easier to implement — though this recommendation is based on five institutions and warrants further study.

A real-world replication using Bakame AI's published llms.txt (bakame.online/llms.txt) confirms both effects on a live, institution-authored file: +45.7pp accuracy lift on covered facts and −12.5pp accuracy drop on uncovered facts — with the harm stronger than on our controlled research documents.

Key terms

  • llms.txt V2 — a structured, machine-readable guide file an institution publishes at /llms.txt so AI systems can read verified information about it. The V2 format adds section headers, scope annotations, and richer formatting over V1.
  • IN_V2 / OUT_V2 — a fact that is (IN) or is not (OUT) explicitly covered by the institution's V2 guide.
  • Injection paradox — the empirical finding that attaching a guide which does not cover a question can make the model more confidently wrong, not just less accurate — because the presence of the guide suppresses honest hedging.
  • Generative Engine Optimization (GEO) — publisher-side practices that shape how AI systems surface information about an institution; the generative-era successor to search engine optimization (SEO).
  • Blind injection — the default llms.txt behavior: the full guide is placed in the model's context for every query regardless of relevance.
  • Query-time gating — checking whether a user's question is in-scope for a guide before deciding to inject it, rather than injecting unconditionally.
  • BM25 + cosine hybrid scorer — a relevance filter combining lexical overlap (BM25) with semantic similarity between sentence embeddings (cosine), used by OPT2 to decide whether a question is in-scope.
  • Fact probe — a short, natural-language question constructed to test whether a model can state a specific ground-truth fact.
  • LLM-as-judge — the methodology of using one language model (here GPT-4o-mini) as an automated scorer of another model's outputs against a reference.
  • In-family bias — the tendency of an LLM judge to agree more with human raters when evaluating outputs from its own training family (GPT, Gemini) than from other families (Claude, Llama, Qwen).
  • Cohen's κ (kappa) — chance-corrected agreement between two raters on a nominal scale; ranges from −1 to 1. Landis & Koch bands: <0.20 slight, 0.21–0.40 fair, 0.41–0.60 moderate, 0.61–0.80 substantial, 0.81–1.00 almost perfect.
  • Krippendorff's α (alpha) — multi-rater reliability metric that tolerates missing data; conventional thresholds 0.667 (tentative) and 0.80 (acceptable).
  • Cohen's h — effect-size measure for the difference between two proportions; |h|≥0.8 is considered a large effect.
  • Percentage points (pp) — the arithmetic difference between two percentages. +60.6pp means an increase from, e.g., 27.6% to 88.2%, not a 60% relative increase.

II. Background and Motivation

A. The llms.txt Standard

The llms.txt standard [8], proposed by Howard and Answer.AI, defines a machine-readable file — a structured text guide — served at /llms.txt that institutions publish so AI systems can read verified information about them. A V2 document extends the original V1 format with section headers, annotations (short labels that describe what each section is for), and richer formatting designed to improve how well AI models understand the content.

The specification says nothing about how AI systems should decide when to use the file at inference time (the moment an AI answers a question). The assumed behavior — and what current implementations do — is full-document injection: the entire guide is given to the AI as background context for every question, regardless of what that question is about.

B. The Blind Injection Problem

Giving the AI the full guide for every question is the opposite of how well-designed AI retrieval systems work. In Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) [11] — the mainstream approach for giving AI models access to external knowledge — context is never given blindly. Every incoming question is first scored for relevance against a knowledge base; only the relevant sections are retrieved and shown to the AI. RAG is the standard in every production AI system that uses external documents.

llms.txt, as currently specified, skips this relevance check. The full guide is given to the AI regardless of whether the user's question falls within what the guide covers. Our Study 1 [1] confirmed that this blind approach improves accuracy on covered facts. Study 2 asks what happens on facts the guide does not cover.

C. Related Work

Prior work establishes that irrelevant context in a model's input degrades factual accuracy. Shi et al. [3] show that LLMs are easily distracted by irrelevant information in the prompt. Cuconasu et al. [4] demonstrate that injecting noisy retrieved documents actively harms RAG performance. Sun, Bai, and Dredze [5] show that once a model has seen contextual information — even incorrect or out-of-scope information — it cannot fully disregard it ("what is seen cannot be unseen"). Singh et al. [7] show that chunk-level relevance filtering before injection outperforms document-level RAG, providing the theoretical basis for our gating approach.

A separate line of work addresses the reliability of LLM-as-judge evaluation, on which this study depends. Zheng et al. [13] documented systematic position and verbosity biases in pairwise LLM judging and reported that strong LLM judges reach approximately 80% agreement with human raters on chat-quality tasks. Panickssery, Bowman, and Feng [14] show that LLM evaluators can distinguish their own generations from those of other models and exhibit a causal self-preference bias — scoring their own outputs more favorably. Section X of this paper quantifies this effect for GPT-4o-mini as the judge of five-model output and reports a per-family κ gap of approximately 0.25.

Generative Engine Optimization (GEO) [2] studies how publishers can optimize content for AI retrieval — but does not address what happens when that content is selectively injected across queries of varying scope. This study addresses that gap.

B. Fact Probe Methodology

For each institution, we compiled a set of verified ground-truth facts from public sources (official websites, press releases, regulatory filings). Each fact was classified as IN_V2 (explicitly covered by the institution's V2 document) or OUT_V2 (not covered, though factually true). A natural-language probe question was written for each fact.

Each model was prompted with each probe question and its response was evaluated by GPT-4o-mini using a structured judge prompt with three verdicts: (A) CORRECT — response accurately states the fact; (B) INCORRECT — response states something wrong; (C) NOT_ADDRESSED — response does not address the fact. Factual accuracy is the proportion of CORRECT verdicts.

IV. Finding 1 — V2 Appears to Improve Factual Accuracy on Covered Facts

Across all five institutions and five models, giving the AI the V2 guide improved how often it got the right answer on facts the guide covers — by an average of +60.6 percentage points (pooled, meaning averaged across all five institutions; p<.001, Cohen's h=+1.452, a large effect by standard statistical measures). This is consistent with Study 1's finding, replicated here with a stricter accuracy metric and a more diverse institution sample.

The improvement was consistent across all five AI models tested, with no model showing a negative result on covered facts. This suggests that a well-structured V2 llms.txt guide can reliably transfer factual information to AI responses — at least when the guide covers the relevant fact and the test conditions match those used here.

The +60.6pp in-scope improvement is large and consistent across institutions and models in our dataset. The open question is what happens when the guide does not cover the fact being asked about.

V. Finding 2 — Incomplete Coverage May Degrade Out-of-Scope Accuracy

On facts not covered by the V2 guide, giving the AI the guide appeared to reduce accuracy compared to giving it nothing — in 4 of 5 institutions. Averaged across all institutions, the accuracy drop was −6.4 percentage points (p=0.001, h=−0.154 — modest in size but statistically significant, meaning it is unlikely to be due to chance alone, and it pointed in the same direction in nearly all institutions tested).

The mechanism is contextual interference — when the AI receives a guide that discusses topics related to, but not exactly matching, a question, it uses that nearby information anyway, often producing a wrong answer instead of saying it doesn't know. Research on knowledge conflict [5] and context distraction [3] shows that AI models cannot selectively ignore context they have already read — it influences their answers even when it is not directly relevant.

Mayo Clinic is the one exception (+1.5pp improvement). The Mayo V2 guide uses sufficiently specialized medical language that questions about uncovered topics receive little contamination from the guide's content. This suggests the size of the harm depends on how closely related the uncovered facts are to what the guide does cover.

An institution that publishes a llms.txt covering some of its operations should expect that document to actively harm AI accuracy on the topics it does not cover. Publishing an incomplete document is not neutral — it is harmful.

VI. Finding 3 — Prompt-Level Fixes Fail

We tested three prompt-level interventions designed to instruct the model to use the V2 document only when relevant:

  • FIX1: "Use the reference document only if it is directly relevant to the question."
  • FIX2: "If the document does not address the question, ignore it and answer from your knowledge."
  • FIX3: Combined FIX1 + FIX2 with explicit scope language.

Figure 3. OUT_V2 accuracy under baseline (A), full V2 injection, and three prompt-fix conditions. FIX conditions do not consistently recover baseline accuracy and worsen the harm for some institutions (notably Mayo Clinic).

None of the three fixes consistently recover accuracy on uncovered facts. For Mayo Clinic, FIX2 and FIX3 reduce accuracy further — the explicit instruction appears to draw the AI's attention to the mismatch between the guide and the question, causing additional confusion rather than appropriate uncertainty.

This is consistent with the "what is seen cannot be unseen" principle demonstrated by Sun, Bai, and Dredze [5]: once an AI model has read a document, that document influences its output regardless of instructions to ignore it. The fix cannot live inside the prompt — it must be applied before the AI sees the guide at all.

In our experiments, instructing the AI to selectively ignore the document after it had already been given did not appear to work reliably. This suggests the intervention may need to happen before the guide reaches the AI — rather than as an instruction inside the prompt.

VII. Finding 4 — Query-Time Gating Recovers Accuracy Without Hurting Recall

In our experiments, the most promising approach was to apply a relevance filter (called RAG-style query-time gating) to llms.txt injection. Rather than giving the AI the full guide for every question, the system first scores each incoming question for relevance — and only gives the AI the guide if the question appears to fall within what the guide covers. The question arrives first; the filter scores it; the guide is shown to the AI only if relevant. The AI never sees the filtering decision itself.

This is not a new technique. It is how every production AI system that uses external documents already works. The novelty here is applying it to llms.txt — a format that was designed under the assumption of always giving the AI the full file.

A. OPT2: Scope-Aware Injection

OPT2 uses two scoring methods together — keyword matching (BM25) and meaning similarity (cosine similarity) — to decide whether an incoming question is relevant to the guide. Each section of the V2 guide has a short label written as (use for: ...) in its header. When a question arrives, OPT2 scores it against those labels. If the best match score exceeds 0.35, the full guide is given to the AI. If not, the AI answers from its own knowledge without seeing the guide.

Figure 0b. OPT2 query-time gating pipeline — the query is scored before the model sees anything.

B. OPT3: Section-Level Retrieval

OPT3 takes a different approach: instead of giving the AI the entire guide, it picks only the top 2 most relevant sections and gives those. The idea is to give the AI less, so it sees only what it needs. In practice, two sections turned out to be too few to cover most in-scope questions.

OPT2 reduced the accuracy drop on uncovered facts in 3 out of 5 institutions. Across all institutions combined, uncovered-fact accuracy was still 5.0pp lower than no guide at all (p=0.0098) — the filter is not perfect, because some questions slip through even when they shouldn't. VIII. Threshold Tuning and Classifier Experiments

A. Threshold Sweep (T20–T45)

The threshold is the sensitivity cutoff that decides whether a question is relevant enough to get the guide. We tested five different cutoff values (0.20, 0.25, 0.30, 0.40, 0.45) in addition to the original 0.35, across all five institutions. Results below are averaged across institutions.

No single cutoff was best at everything. Lower cutoffs (like T25) let more questions through to get the guide, which is good for covered facts but allows more uncovered questions to slip in too. Higher cutoffs (like T40) block more questions, which is better for uncovered facts but risks blocking some that should have gotten the guide. The original 0.35 cutoff fell within 1–2 percentage points of the best setting on both measures, suggesting it is a reasonable starting point. Results varied by institution, which points to a possible improvement: rather than one cutoff for everyone, a system that learns the right cutoff per institution would likely do better.

B. Top-k Sweep (K3–K5)

OPT3 is a different strategy: instead of giving the AI the full guide or nothing, it picks the most relevant guide sections and gives only those. We tested giving 2, 3, 4, or 5 sections (k=2 through k=5).

The problem: 2 sections is not enough. The AI misses most of the covered facts because they are spread across many sections of the guide. Giving it 5 sections gets closer to full accuracy on covered facts — but then we are almost back to giving the AI the whole guide, which reintroduces the harm on uncovered facts.

There is no sweet spot. Every value of k either misses too many covered facts or lets in too many uncovered ones. The relevance filter (OPT2) handled this tradeoff better than selecting sections did.

C. Real-World Replication — Bakame Published llms.txt

Every experiment so far used guide documents we wrote ourselves for each institution. That raises a fair question: do the same results hold when using a real llms.txt file that an institution actually published? To check, we downloaded Bakame AI's live guide from bakame.online/llms.txt (as of October 2026) and ran the same set of test questions on it.

The real file turned out to cover slightly more facts than our synthetic version — 21 out of 29 test facts, compared to 19 out of 29 for the version we wrote. Two facts in the live file were not in ours: details about Bakame Aviation's English proficiency standard, and the shared Kinyarwanda voice-AI setup.

D. Scope Classifier Experiments (OPT4 — OPT4_263)

OPT2 works, but it requires two things: section labels written in a specific format (use for: ...) and a manually chosen cutoff value. Both require effort to set up for each new institution. We wanted to know if a machine learning model could learn to make that relevance decision automatically — without needing the labels or the manual tuning. We ran four experiments (OPT4, OPT4v2, OPT5, OPT4_263), each trying a slightly different approach.

OPT4 — Training a small AI model to decide relevance. We trained DistilBERT — a small, fast text-classification model — on 233 labeled examples to predict whether a given question is in scope for the guide. To check how well it generalizes, we trained on four institutions and tested on the fifth, repeating this five times (one per institution). Accuracy on uncovered facts averaged 29.9%, roughly matching the no-guide baseline (29.5%). The downside: it was too aggressive at blocking questions, so accuracy on covered facts dropped to 71.5% — well below the 88.2% achieved by always giving the guide.

OPT4v2 — Adding AI-generated training examples. We tried giving OPT4 more training data by having an AI label 273 additional examples, bringing the total to 506. More data usually helps — but here it made things worse. Accuracy on uncovered facts dropped to 26.2% and covered-fact accuracy fell to 76.8%. Instead of DistilBERT, we tried a lighter approach: convert each question into a numerical meaning vector (sentence embedding), then train a simple statistical classifier on top. IX. Failure Mode Analysis — Why Do Uncovered Facts Fail?

Section V established that giving the AI the guide reduces accuracy on uncovered facts. This section asks a more precise question: how does the AI fail — does it give a wrong answer confidently, or does it simply say it doesn't know? And which types of facts are most likely to be missing from an institution's guide in the first place?

We analyzed 2,604 verdict records across all five institutions and both baseline conditions (A = no guide, V2 = guide injected), using the three-way verdict system: A = correct, B = wrong answer stated confidently, C = fact not addressed (the AI hedged or said it didn't know).

A. The Injection Paradox: Confident Wrong Answers Increase

Without the guide, AI models fail on uncovered facts by giving a wrong answer 48.1% of the time and declining to answer 23.6% of the time. When the guide is given, the picture shifts dramatically: wrong answers climb to 70.7% while non-answers drop to just 6.8%.

ConditionnCorrect (A)Wrong / confident (B)Not addressed (C)
No guide (A)64528.4%48.1%23.6%
With guide (V2)62222.5%70.7%6.8%

Giving the AI the guide suppresses uncertainty. The model reads the guide, finds content that is nearby but not identical to the question, and produces a confident wrong answer instead of admitting it doesn't know. To quantify this directly, we matched each fact×model pair across both conditions and tracked how the verdict changed:

Transition (No guide → With guide)Count%
Wrong → still wrong (B → B)27043.4%
Silent → confidently wrong (C → B)9014.5%
Correct → still correct (A → A)8914.3%
Correct → wrong (A → B)8012.9%
Wrong → correct (B → A)345.5%
Silent → correct (C → A)172.7%

In 14.5% of uncovered-fact cases, the guide converted a silent non-answer into a confident wrong answer. In 12.9% of cases, the guide turned a correct answer into a wrong one. The guide fixed wrong answers (B→A + C→A) in only 8.2% of cases. The net effect: the guide makes things worse on uncovered facts not by subtly degrading accuracy, but by actively suppressing the AI's natural uncertainty.

In our experiments, the guide didn't just fail to help on uncovered facts — it appeared to make the AI more confidently wrong. This pattern, which we call the injection paradox, suggests that the same document that improves accuracy on covered facts may actively suppress appropriate uncertainty on uncovered ones. Whether this generalizes beyond this study warrants further investigation.

We then checked which of those facts actually appeared in each institution's llms.txt guide. X. Human Validation of the Automated Judge

A. Motivation

Sections IV–IX rely on GPT-4o-mini as an automated accuracy judge over all 14,500 model generations. This design decision is practical — human adjudication at that scale is infeasible — but it introduces an evaluator-level confound: if the judge's agreement with human assessment varies systematically across model families, downstream per-model comparisons inherit that bias. To bound this risk, we conducted a human-validation sub-study on a stratified sample of 300 generations drawn across the full institution × model × condition grid.

B. Sub-study design

Instrument. We built a lightweight web application (burnettbench-grader.vercel.app) that presents each item as a tuple of (question, ground-truth fact, model response) with no reference to the model identity, the condition, the automated judge's label, or the fact's in-scope status. Graders issue a five-way verdict: (A) correct, (B) confidently wrong, (C) not addressed, (D) flag ground truth as suspect, (S) skip. Keyboard shortcuts enforce single-keystroke submission. Per-item engaged time is tracked with tab-visibility pausing and a 180-second per-item cap.

Sample. The 300 items were stratified across 5 institutions × 5 models × 12 conditions, with replacement at the cell level to ensure each stratum received at least one item. GPT-4o-mini's blind judgments on these items were stored separately (service-role access only) and never shown to graders.

Graders. We recruited 5 independent graders (Rwandan university students and recent graduates). Graders were compensated for their time, capped at 5 hours per grader. Graders worked asynchronously; no cross-rater communication was permitted during the collection window.

Collection. Between 7 and 15 November 2026, we collected 1,000 ratings covering all 300 items. 297 items (99%) received ≥2 independent human ratings; 189 items (63%) received ≥3.

C. Agreement statistics

We report three complementary measures of agreement on the 300-item sample.

Pooled Cohen's κ (humans vs. automated judge). Treating each human rating as an independent comparison against the GPT-4o-mini judge (A/B/C only; FLAG and SKIP excluded; n = 939 comparable ratings):

κpooled = 0.531 ± 0.023    (raw agreement: 68.6%)

Majority-vote Cohen's κ (humans vs. judge). For the 297 items with ≥2 independent human ratings, we take the majority verdict as the human ground truth and compare against the judge:

κmajority = 0.570    (raw agreement: 71.4%, n = 297)

Both estimates fall in the "moderate" band on the Landis & Koch (1977) scale. The judge is correlated with human judgment at meaningful levels but is not a human-equivalent oracle; approximately one in three verdicts diverges from the majority human rating. We treat κmajority as the headline estimate; κpooled is reported for its tighter confidence interval.

Krippendorff's α (inter-human reliability). To assess whether low judge–human agreement reflects judge error or genuine task ambiguity, we compute Krippendorff's α across human raters only (nominal, A/B/C, 297 items with ≥2 ratings):

α = 0.567

α sits in the "below conventional reliability threshold" band (Krippendorff's suggested cutoffs: 0.667 for tentative conclusions, 0.80 for acceptable reliability). The near-identity of α (humans vs. humans, 0.567) and κmajority (humans vs. judge, 0.570) is informative: the judge's disagreement with humans is approximately the same magnitude as humans' disagreement with each other. The ceiling on any single-rater evaluation of this task — human or model — is roughly moderate agreement, not near-perfect. This reflects genuine ambiguity in the A/B/C schema at the boundaries (e.g., a response that is technically accurate but hedges heavily), not a specific pathology of the automated judge.

Decomposing κ by the model whose output is being evaluated reveals a systematic pattern:

The GPT-4o-mini judge shows highest agreement with humans (κ = 0.575) when evaluating outputs from its own training family (GPT) and from the closely-aligned Google/Gemini family (κ = 0.536). Agreement drops by approximately 0.25 κ when evaluating outputs from Claude, Llama, and Qwen — a gap larger than the full standard error of the overall estimate.

We interpret this as in-family evaluation bias, consistent with the self-preference phenomenon documented by Panickssery et al. [14]: LLM judges appear to recognize outputs from their own family and score them more favorably. Our finding extends this observation into the context-coverage evaluation setting, where per-family decompositions of judge–human agreement remain underexplored.

Implication for Sections IV–IX. Per-model accuracy estimates reported in this paper should be interpreted with the caveat that Claude, Llama, and Qwen outputs are evaluated by a judge that agrees with humans less reliably on their output styles. The directional findings (the injection paradox, OPT2/3 recovery, Bakame OUT harm) are robust across all models and are unlikely to be artifacts of judge bias. Per-model ordering within the accuracy tables should not be treated as reliable without multi-judge triangulation.

E. Per-condition and per-institution agreement

Agreement is unevenly distributed across experimental conditions:

Condition classRepresentative κ rangeInterpretation
Fully-contexted (K3, K4, K5, OPT3_kfull, AND40)0.64 – 0.85Judge performs well when model has clear context

The clever-gating conditions (OPT4v2, OPT5, learned classifiers) show the lowest judge–human agreement. These are precisely the conditions where model output can arise from two distinct routing paths (in-scope → full guide; out-of-scope → no guide), producing behavior that is harder to adjudicate without knowing the routing decision. We flag this as a boundary condition of judge reliability.

By institution, κ is uniformly moderate (0.446–0.597), with Bakame lowest (κ = 0.446). We attribute this to the obscurity of Bakame-specific facts: when ground truth is itself hard to verify, humans exhibit higher within-rater variance, mechanically depressing κ regardless of judge performance.

Graders used the ground-truth flag (D) on 5.6% of items (56 of 1,000), identifying cases where the cited fact was unverifiable, ambiguous, or contradicted by linked sources. These items are excluded from κ computation and are released separately as a ground-truth audit set to be addressed in future work. The non-zero flag rate indicates healthy critical engagement rather than rubber-stamping.

F. Sub-study limitations

  • Per-cell power. With 300 items across 300 cells (5 inst × 5 models × 12 cond), per-condition κ carries wide confidence intervals; sub-group findings are directional, not point estimates.
  • Single-judge triangulation. Only GPT-4o-mini served as the automated judge. A multi-judge design (adding Claude and Gemini as co-judges) would allow direct estimation of judge-family bias independent of the human ground truth.
  • Grader population. All graders are Rwandan university-educated English speakers. Generalization to other grader populations is unverified.
  • Task difficulty ceiling. The near-identity of inter-human Krippendorff α (0.567) and majority-vote human-vs-judge κ (0.570) indicates that the A/B/C verdict schema itself admits roughly one in three boundary disagreements even among expert human raters. Any judge (automated or human) will exhibit moderate rather than high agreement on this task; the per-model bias of ≈0.25 κ documented in Section X.D is observed above this ambient noise floor.

XI. Spec Recommendation — covers: and excludes: Fields

The llms.txt specification [8] says nothing about how AI systems should decide when to use the file at the moment of answering a question (inference time). The current design — a static file served at /llms.txt — provides no mechanism for question-aware filtering. An AI system following the spec as written would give the AI the full file for every question it receives.

Our relevance filter (OPT2) requires (use for: ...) labels in section headers — scope signals we manually added to our research documents, and which Bakame AI independently added to their real published file. The fact that both a controlled research document and a real production document converged on this same labeling pattern suggests that publishers intuitively recognize the need for scope signals. The specification should formalize this practice.

We propose two new metadata fields in the llms.txt specification:

Figure 0c. Proposed publisher-declared scope: covers: and excludes: fields enable consumer-side gating without custom classifiers.

# llms.txt — proposed extension

> covers: admissions, financial aid,
  student life, academic programs

> excludes: research funding, faculty
  credentials, endowment, hiring

> audience: prospective undergraduate students

> scope-version: 1.0

The covers: field declares the topics the document addresses. The excludes: field explicitly marks topics that are intentionally absent. The audience: field scopes the query population the document is optimized for. Together, these fields enable AI consumers to implement query-time gating without engineering their own scope classifiers — the publisher declares scope, the consumer enforces it.

This is not a new architectural concept. It mirrors how robots.txt's Disallow: field works for crawlers: the publisher declares what is out of scope; the consumer is expected to respect that declaration. The parallel is intentional — llms.txt is positioned as an AI-era successor to robots.txt, and should inherit its publisher-declared scope model.

If the specification adopts covers: and excludes:, the OPT2 relevance filter becomes substantially simpler: instead of inferring scope from annotation text, the filter reads the publisher-declared topics directly. This reduces the engineering burden on AI consumers from "build a scope classifier" to "read a field."

XII. Practical Guidance

The key insight from this study is that coverage completeness is relative to the intended use case, not to the totality of knowable facts about an institution. A complete llms.txt for an enrollment goal means every question a prospective student is likely to ask is answered — not every fact about the university.

Institution typePrimary audienceTopics to coverRisk if incomplete
UniversityProspective studentsAdmissions, financial aid, housing, programsLow if query volume outside scope is low
Hospital / ClinicPatients, referrersServices, locations, insurance, wait timesMedium — health queries are high stakes
Law firmClients, recruitsPractice areas, offices, compensationHigh if financial/legal data omitted
Government agencyPublic / applicantsFees, forms, processing times, eligibilityHigh — very broad query surface
AI startupInvestors, partnersProduct, team, awards, accessLow — undisclosed items are often intentional

Based on our preliminary findings, we offer four tentative suggestions. These are not prescriptions — they are starting points drawn from a single study with five institutions, and further research is needed before they can be treated as established guidance.

Preliminary suggestion 1 — Think about scope before writing. It may help to define who will be asking questions and what they are likely to ask before writing the guide. A document written for a specific audience and use case appears more likely to be complete enough to avoid the harm we observed — though this has not been tested directly.

Preliminary suggestion 2 — Be cautious with incomplete guides. In our experiments, a guide that did not fully cover its topic appeared to reduce AI accuracy on uncovered questions. If a guide cannot cover the intended use case reasonably well, it may be worth waiting until relevance filtering (gating) is in place before publishing. This is a caution, not a rule — real-world conditions will vary.

Preliminary suggestion 3 — Query-time filtering may be worth exploring. Our OPT2 experiment suggests that checking whether a question is relevant to the guide before deciding to give it to the AI could help reduce harm on uncovered facts, while mostly preserving accuracy on covered ones. More testing across different institutions, file formats, and AI systems would be needed before this could be broadly recommended.

Preliminary suggestion 4 — The llms.txt spec may benefit from scope declarations. In our experiments, adding short scope labels to guide sections (use for: ...) was what made OPT2's relevance filter work. That is essentially what covers: and excludes: fields would do at the document level — declare scope so AI consumers don't have to guess it. We tested the mechanism; what we have not tested is how it would work if built into the spec itself and adopted by real AI tools at scale. That remains an open question.

XIII. Limitations and Future Work

A. Study limitations

L1 — Judge reliability (partially addressed in Section X). Using GPT-4o-mini as the automated accuracy judge introduces potential bias. The human-validation sub-study in Section X quantifies this directly: majority-vote agreement with humans sits at κ = 0.570 (moderate), with a per-model family gap of approximately 0.25 κ (highest on GPT-family outputs, lowest on Llama). Directional findings in Sections IV–IX are robust, but per-model ordering within accuracy tables should be interpreted with the in-family bias caveat. Multi-judge triangulation — adding Claude and Gemini as independent co-judges — remains open future work.

L2 — Threshold generalization. The threshold sweep (Section VIII) shows no universally optimal sensitivity setting — T25 through T40 perform within 2pp on averaged metrics, but institution-level variation is high. A classifier that learns per-institution thresholds from a small set of labeled examples (domain-adaptive) would be preferable to the single global threshold used here.

L3 — OPT3 ceiling. Section-level retrieval recovers recall only partially at K5 (85.1% vs OPT2's 86.8%) while not improving OUT_V2 accuracy. Improved section chunking, re-ranking, or multi-vector retrieval may unlock the approach. The current implementation is not viable below K5 and is outperformed by threshold gating at all k values tested.

L4 — No real-world consumer measurement. All experiments simulate inference-time injection via system prompt. Real AI consumer behavior — whether Perplexity, ChatGPT, Cursor, or other tools fetch and inject llms.txt automatically — was not measured directly. Confirmed automatic llms.txt adoption remains low as of October 2026; the harm documented here is prospective, not observed in production.

L5 — Five institutions. Domain diversity is reasonable but not exhaustive. Replication with additional institution types (e.g., retail, non-profit, media) is needed before claims of universality. The Mayo Clinic exception (positive OUT_V2 direction) warrants further investigation.

L6 — English only. All V2 documents and probe questions are in English. Cross-lingual effects on scope harm are unknown. The effect may be larger or smaller in languages with weaker parametric knowledge coverage.

L7 — Synthetic documents. Four of five institutions use researcher-authored V2 documents, not published files. The real-world replication (Bakame) showed stronger harm on the published file, suggesting synthetic documents may underestimate the production effect.

B. Threat model and adversarial future work

Scope of this paper. All findings reported in Sections IV–X assume an honest-publisher threat model: the institution authoring the llms.txt guide intends to provide accurate information about itself, does not attempt to manipulate model behavior for third-party benefit, and does not include adversarial content. Under this assumption, the injection paradox (Section IX.A) is a performance failure — the model becomes more confidently wrong on uncovered facts due to representational dynamics in context processing, not due to deliberate manipulation. This assumption is load-bearing. The empirical results of this paper apply only when it holds.

The adversarial surface. The same injection channel that produces benign accuracy harm constitutes a broad attack surface under an adversarial-publisher threat model. Preliminary analysis suggests at least seven distinct attack classes merit empirical measurement:

ClassMechanismRepresentative target outcome
T1 — Direct overrideGuide contains explicit instructions overriding the user prompt (Perez & Ribeiro [16])Model issues attacker-controlled response to arbitrary queries
T2 — Instructional driftSubtle persuasive framing shifts model behavior without explicit overrideReduced hedging; biased framing of competitors
T3 — Data exfiltrationGuide instructs inclusion of attacker-controlled URLs or markdown images (Greshake et al. [15])User queries silently leaked via image/URL loaders
T4 — Brand redirectionGuide maps questions about X to recommendations for competitor YMarket manipulation at inference time
T5 — Jailbreak amplificationGuide carries latent jailbreak templates (Zou et al. [17])Safety guardrails bypassed on specific user queries
T6 — Hallucination engineeringDeliberate omission exploits the injection paradox (Section IX.A)Attacker-engineered confident-wrong responses on specific facts
T7 — Delayed / multi-stageGuide primes context that activates on an attacker's follow-up queryMulti-turn attacks invisible in single-turn evaluation

Interaction with the gating defenses in this paper. The scope-gating methods evaluated in Sections VII and VIII (OPT2 through OPT5) were motivated by accuracy recovery on out-of-scope questions. We hypothesize — but do not test in this paper — that they also function as a first line of security defense: OPT2 (BM25 + cosine gating) may filter out adversarial guides whose attack payload lacks lexical overlap with the user query; OPT4 / OPT5 (learned scope classifiers) may be adaptable as direct injection-detection classifiers trained on adversarial-vs-benign guide pairs. The hypothesis cuts the other way as well: an adversary who knows the gating method can tune attack payloads to survive the gate (e.g., embedding attack content in topically-relevant passages). The resulting attack-success-rate × defense-coverage matrix is the subject of planned follow-on work.

Explicit non-claims. We do not claim that (1) the gating methods in this paper constitute adequate security defenses as deployed; (2) the llms.txt V2 standard should be adopted without security review; or (3) the injection paradox implies the adversarial case — adversarial behavior must be measured directly. Institutions adopting the standard, and model providers ingesting llms.txt-style guides in production, should treat the adversarial threat model as an open question.

Study 3 — adversarial empirics. We are executing a follow-on study (BurnettBench Study 3: Adversarial llms.txt Empirics) that measures (1) baseline attack success rates across all seven classes × five target models; (2) whether the Sections VII–VIII gating methods incidentally reduce attack success; (3) the precision/recall ceiling of a dedicated adversarial-guide detector trained on hand-crafted attack corpora. Study 3 adopts a responsible-disclosure protocol: adversarial examples will be disclosed to affected model providers 90 days prior to public release of the attack corpus.

XIV. What Can and Cannot Be Concluded

This study offers preliminary evidence that an accurate, well-structured llms.txt V2 guide may reduce AI accuracy on questions it does not cover. In our experiments, the effect appeared statistically significant (p=0.001, meaning unlikely to be due to chance alone), was observed across 4 of 5 institutions, and persisted after prompt-level interventions. It also appeared in a real published llms.txt file, not only in researcher-authored documents.

This finding does not undermine the value of llms.txt. Giving an AI a well-structured guide appeared to dramatically improve accuracy on covered facts (+60.6pp, p<.001), consistent with Study 1 and extending it across five institutions and five models. Rather, the finding suggests a potential structural gap in the current standard: there is no mechanism for institutions to declare what their guide covers, and no mechanism for AI systems to check relevance before using it.

A relevance filter — treating llms.txt as a document to retrieve from selectively rather than a header to inject always (treating it as a RAG corpus rather than a static header) — appeared to substantially reduce accuracy harm on uncovered facts while preserving accuracy on covered ones (−1.3pp, not statistically significant). The approach uses infrastructure similar to what modern AI retrieval pipelines already employ.

The spec extension we propose — covers: and excludes: fields — could complement this approach. It would shift the responsibility for declaring scope from AI systems (who would have to infer it) to publishers (who already know it), potentially making the standard safer to use even when the guide is incomplete — which appears to be common in practice.

The practical takeaway from our experiments: scope your llms.txt to your audience's questions, not to all facts about your institution. Consider filtering at the time of the query. Declare scope in the document. A targeted, complete guide for one use case appeared more valuable — and less harmful — than a broad, incomplete one.
